### Question 1 — Conceptual (Remember)
**[Multiple Choice]**

What happens during a **double-blind** peer review process?

- A) Two reviewers look at the paper, and the editor makes the final decision blindly.
- B) The reviewers know the authors' identities, but the authors do not know the reviewers' identities.
- C) Neither the authors nor the reviewers know each other's identities.
- D) The reviews are published openly alongside the paper without names attached.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**C** — In a double-blind review, author names and affiliations are stripped from the manuscript before it is sent to reviewers, minimizing prestige and gender bias.

</details>

### Question 2 — Conceptual (Understand)
**[Multiple Choice]**

If you submit a paper to a highly respected academic journal, what is the most common outcome for a successful first submission?

- A) Accept as is.
- B) Revise and Resubmit (R&R).
- C) Minor Revisions with immediate publication.
- D) Re-route to a conference.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**B** — "Revise and Resubmit" (R&R) is the standard positive outcome. It means the editor and reviewers see value in the work but require specific changes or additional experiments before it can be accepted.

</details>

### Question 3 — Scenario-Based (Apply)
**[Multiple Choice]**

A PhD student in Artificial Intelligence has a groundbreaking new neural network architecture. To maximize their career prospects and prestige in their specific field, where should they submit their work?

- A) A traditional, slow-moving biology journal.
- B) A top-tier, highly selective peer-reviewed Computer Science conference (e.g., NeurIPS).
- C) An un-reviewed preprint server only.
- D) A general-interest science magazine.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**B** — In Computer Science and AI, top-tier conferences are the gold standard for publication, often carrying as much or more prestige than traditional journals due to the fast pace of the field.

</details>

### Question 4 — Scenario-Based (Apply)
**[Short Answer]**

A researcher has published 5 papers. Their citation counts are: 100, 50, 4, 2, and 1. What is their h-index, and why?

<details>
<summary>Sample Answer</summary>

Their **h-index is 3**. 
If we sort the papers:
1. 100 ( $\ge$ 1)
2. 50 ( $\ge$ 2)
3. 4 ( $\ge$ 3)
4. 2 (Not $\ge$ 4. Stop here).
They have exactly 3 papers that have been cited at least 3 times. 

</details>

### Question 5 — Conceptual (Analyze)
**[Multiple Choice]**

Which of the following is an example of Goodhart's Law ("When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") in academic publishing?

- A) A researcher publishing a paper at a conference because the field moves quickly.
- B) A journal editor rejecting a paper because the methodology is flawed.
- C) An author artificially inflating their citation count by citing their own previous, unrelated papers in every new article they write.
- D) A university hiring a professor based on a teaching demonstration.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**C** — The citation count is meant to be a measure of a paper's usefulness to the community. When the author forces self-citations just to boost their h-index (treating the measure as a target), the metric loses its meaning.

</details>
