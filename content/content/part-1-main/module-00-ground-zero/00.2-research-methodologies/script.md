**[Audio / Voiceover]**

## Scene 2: The Context (2–3 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- Slide showing three pillars: Quantitative (Numbers), Qualitative (Words), Mixed Methods (Both).

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"At the highest level, scientific inquiry is divided into three major paradigms: Quantitative, Qualitative, and Mixed Methods. 

**Quantitative research** is the language of numbers. It's about objective measurement, statistics, and deductive logic—starting with a theory and testing it. If you are running randomized controlled trials, analyzing census data, or doing A/B testing, you are in the quantitative world.

**Qualitative research** is the language of words and meaning. It's about subjective human experience and inductive logic—observing the world to build new theories. If you are conducting in-depth interviews, doing ethnographic field work, or analyzing historical texts, you are in the qualitative world.

And **Mixed Methods** is exactly what it sounds like: combining both to get a more complete picture."

---

## Scene 3: The Concept (3–5 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- Split screen visual: Satellite looking at a forest (Quantitative) vs. a person examining a single leaf closely (Qualitative).

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Think of it like studying a forest. Quantitative research is using a satellite to measure the total biomass of the forest. It gives you massive scale and generalizability, but it lacks deep context. Qualitative research is walking into the forest to study exactly how the local ecosystem interacts around a single tree. It gives you incredible, rich detail, but you can't assume every tree in the forest acts the same way.

How do you choose? The methodology must flow directly from the research question. 

If your question is *'Does X cause Y?'* or *'What percentage of people do Z?'*, you must use a Quantitative approach. 
But if your question is *'How do people experience X?'* or *'Why does Y happen in this specific community?'*, you must use a Qualitative approach. You cannot measure human subjective grief with a 1-to-5 Likert scale, and you cannot prove a drug's efficacy with an anecdote.

Finally, you have to ensure your method is both **Valid** and **Reliable**. Reliability means consistency: if I repeat the study, do I get the same result? Validity means accuracy: am I actually measuring what I claim to be measuring? A broken bathroom scale that always says you weigh 100 pounds is highly reliable, but completely invalid."

## Scene 4: The Demonstration (5–8 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- Whiteboard animation mapping research questions to methodologies.

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Let's look at a real-world scenario. Imagine you are researching a new AI educational app for high school math. 

**Scenario A:** Your core question is: *'Does using this app improve math test scores compared to traditional studying?'*
This is a causal question. The methodology must be Quantitative. You would set up a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) with a control group and an experimental group, and run statistical t-tests on their final exam scores. The gold standard here is statistical significance.

**Scenario B:** Your core question is: *'Why do students stop using the app after two weeks?'*
This is an exploratory 'why' question. An RCT won't help you here. A survey won't give you deep enough answers. The methodology must be Qualitative. You need to conduct semi-structured interviews with 15 students who churned. Your sample size isn't driven by statistical power; it's driven by 'data saturation'—you stop interviewing when new students stop giving you new reasons.

**Scenario C:** You want to know if it improves scores, AND why some students love it while others hate it. 
This requires Mixed Methods. You run the RCT to prove efficacy (Quantitative), and you follow up with focus groups to understand the user experience (Qualitative). The quantitative data tells you *that* it works; the qualitative data tells you *why* it works. To build a truly great product, you need both."
