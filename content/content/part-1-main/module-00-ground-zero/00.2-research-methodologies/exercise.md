# Exercise: Methodological Matchmaker
**Module:** 0.2 — Research Methodologies
**Estimated Time:** 20 minutes
**Difficulty:** ⭐⭐ Intermediate
**Nexus Tool:** None

---

## Scenario

> You have been hired as a Research Consultant for a university laboratory. Junior students frequently submit research proposals that have a fatal flaw: the methodology they chose does not actually answer the research question they are asking. 
> 
> Your job is to review three flawed proposals, identify the mismatch, and propose the correct methodological approach.

---

## Tasks

### Task 1: The App Evaluation
**Proposal 1:** 
"I want to understand the deep emotional reasons why nursing students drop out of their clinical training programs. I am going to email a multiple-choice survey with a 1-to-5 Likert scale to 500 nursing students across the country."

**Your Job:**
1. What is the core research question?
2. What paradigm did the student choose?
3. Why will this fail (explain the mismatch)?
4. What methodology *should* they use?

---

### Task 2: The Efficacy Claim
**Proposal 2:** 
"I want to prove that our new 'Focus-State' meditation technique objectively lowers resting heart rate better than traditional mindfulness meditation. I am going to conduct in-depth, 2-hour interviews with 5 people who tried the new technique and ask them how relaxed they felt."

**Your Job:**
1. What is the core research question?
2. What paradigm did the student choose?
3. Why will this fail (explain the mismatch)?
4. What methodology *should* they use?

---

### Task 3: The Broken Scale
**Proposal 3:**
"I want to measure the coding ability of computer science students. I created a test that asks them 50 questions about the history of the Apple Macintosh computer. I gave the test to the same 100 students on Monday, and again on Friday, and they all got the exact same scores both times. Therefore, my test is a perfect research instrument."

**Your Job:**
1. Using the concepts of **Reliability** and **Validity**, evaluate this student's claim. Is the test reliable? Is it valid? Explain why.

---

## Reflection

Notice how easy it is to fall back on methods we are comfortable with (like a quick online survey) even when they are inappropriate for the task. The hardest part of research design isn't doing the math; it's making sure you are measuring the right thing in the right way.

### Task 1 Solution
1. **Question:** "Why do nursing students drop out?" (Exploratory, 'why', meaning-making).
2. **Chosen Paradigm:** Quantitative (Likert scale survey).
3. **Mismatch:** You cannot capture deep, nuanced emotional reasons using a 1-to-5 scale. A survey forces respondents into predefined boxes and misses the subjective human experience.
4. **Correction:** Qualitative approach. The student should conduct semi-structured interviews with 15-20 students who dropped out to explore their specific, subjective experiences.

### Task 2 Solution
1. **Question:** "Does Technique A lower heart rate better than Technique B?" (Causal, objective measurement).
2. **Chosen Paradigm:** Qualitative (Interviews).
3. **Mismatch:** The student is trying to prove an objective, physiological cause-and-effect relationship using small-sample subjective anecdotes.
4. **Correction:** Quantitative approach (specifically, a Randomized Controlled Trial). They need a control group (traditional meditation) and an experimental group (new technique), and they need to objectively measure heart rate (BPM) across a statistically significant sample size to run a t-test.

### Task 3 Solution
- **Reliability:** The test IS highly reliable. Reliability just means consistency. Because the students got the exact same scores on Monday and Friday, the instrument is consistent.
- **Validity:** The test IS NOT valid. Validity means accuracy—are you measuring what you claim to measure? The student claims to be measuring "coding ability," but the test actually measures "Apple trivia knowledge." Therefore, the instrument is completely invalid for the research question.
