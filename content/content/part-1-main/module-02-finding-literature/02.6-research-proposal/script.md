# Module 2.6: Writing a Research Proposal
**Series:** Modern Research Methodologies
**Module:** Finding Literature — The Search Phase

---

## Scene 1: The Hook (≤ 30 seconds)

**[Visual]**
- A student staring at a blank grant application form. The deadline is in 14 days. The word count: 2,000 words. The funding amount: $150,000.

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"A grant proposal. Two thousand words. Fourteen days. One hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

Most PhD students treat writing a research proposal like writing a longer version of an abstract. They describe what they want to do. They get rejected. The reason? Reviewers don't fund *what* you're going to do. They fund *why it matters* and *why you are the person to do it*.

Today, we learn how to write a proposal that gets funded."

---

## Scene 2: The Heilmeier Catechism, Extended (3–4 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- The 7 questions of the Heilmeier Catechism appear one by one on screen.

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"In Module 2.1, we used the Heilmeier Catechism to design a research question. Now we use it to build an entire proposal around that question.

George Heilmeier was the Director of DARPA. He demanded that every research pitch answer seven questions before he'd fund it:

One: What are you trying to do? In plain English, no jargon.
Two: How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?
Three: What is new in your approach, and why do you think it will succeed?
Four: Who cares? If you succeed, what difference does it make?
Five: What are the risks and the payoffs?
Six: How much will it cost? How long will it take?
Seven: What are the mid-term and final 'exams' to check for success?

These seven questions ARE your proposal structure. Every section of your grant maps directly to one of these questions."

---

## Scene 3: The Logic Model (2–3 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- A horizontal flowchart: Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Funders think in terms of a Logic Model. They want to trace a direct line from the money they give you, to the change it creates in the world.

Inputs are your resources: time, equipment, data. Activities are what you do: run experiments, conduct interviews. Outputs are what you produce: papers, datasets, a trained model. Outcomes are the near-term effects: a new clinical guideline, a better algorithm. Impact is the long-term vision: fewer cancer deaths, cleaner energy.

Your proposal must make this chain of causation completely obvious. If a reviewer can't trace the money to the impact, they reject it."

---

## Scene 4: Structure of a Winning Proposal (3–5 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- A document outline expands section by section.

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Most grant proposals share a standard five-section structure:

**Section 1: Significance.** Why does this problem matter? What happens if nobody solves it? This maps to Heilmeier Questions 1, 2, and 4.

**Section 2: Innovation.** What is new about your approach? How is it different from everything tried before? This maps to Heilmeier Question 3.

**Section 3: Approach.** The methodology. How will you actually do this? Timeline, milestones, contingency plans. This maps to Heilmeier Questions 6 and 7.

**Section 4: Team.** Why are *you* the right person to do this? List your relevant experience, publications, and collaborators.

**Section 5: Budget Justification.** Every line item must be justified. 'Laptop: $2,000 — needed to run computational models.' Never leave a reviewer wondering why you need something."

---

## Scene 5: The SMART Objectives (2 minutes)

**[Visual]**
- The SMART acronym fills in letter by letter: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Your objectives section is where most proposals die. Students write vague objectives like 'To investigate the impact of X on Y.'

A SMART objective is: 'To measure the effect of intervention X on outcome Y in population Z, using methodology M, within 18 months, with a target effect size of D.'

Reviewers are pattern-matching for vagueness. SMART objectives signal that you have actually thought through your research design."

---

## Scene 6: Summary (≤ 30 seconds)

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Let's recap:
1. **Heilmeier Catechism:** Seven questions structure your entire proposal.
2. **Logic Model:** Trace a clear line from funding to real-world impact.
3. **Standard Structure:** Significance → Innovation → Approach → Team → Budget.
4. **SMART Objectives:** Be specific, measurable, and time-bound. Never vague."

---

## Scene 7: Standout Close

**[Audio / Voiceover]**
"Reviewers don't fund ideas. They fund confidence. A well-structured proposal says: 'I have thought about every possible objection you might have, and I have an answer for it.'"
