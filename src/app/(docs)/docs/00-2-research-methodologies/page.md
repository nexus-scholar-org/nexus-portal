## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

1. **Define** the differences between quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods research paradigms.
2. **Identify** appropriate methodologies for different types of research questions.
3. **Evaluate** the strengths and limitations of each major methodology.
4. **Explain** key concepts of validity, reliability, and bias in research design.

---

## Key Concepts

### Concept 1: The Research Paradigms
- **What it is:** The foundational approaches to research. 
  - *Quantitative:* Focuses on numbers, statistics, and objective measurement (e.g., surveys, experiments). Deductive approach (testing theories).
  - *Qualitative:* Focuses on words, meanings, and subjective experiences (e.g., interviews, ethnography). Inductive approach (building theories).
  - *Mixed Methods:* Combining both to provide a more complete picture.
- **Why it matters:** Choosing the wrong paradigm for a research question guarantees a flawed study. You cannot measure human subjective grief with a Likert scale, and you cannot prove a drug's efficacy with an anecdote.
- **Analogy:** Quantitative research is looking at a forest with a satellite to measure total biomass. Qualitative research is walking into the forest to study a specific tree up close. Mixed methods is doing both to understand the ecosystem.

### Concept 2: Matching Methodology to the Question
- **What it is:** The methodology must flow directly from the research question.
  - "How many?" or "Does X cause Y?" $\rightarrow$ Quantitative (Experimental, Correlational).
  - "How do people experience X?" or "Why does Y happen?" $\rightarrow$ Qualitative (Phenomenology, Grounded Theory).
- **Why it matters:** A common mistake for junior researchers is picking a method they are comfortable with (like an online survey) before clearly defining what they need to know.

### Concept 3: Validity and Reliability
- **What it is:** 
  - *Reliability:* Consistency. If you repeat the study, do you get the same result?
  - *Validity:* Accuracy. Are you actually measuring what you claim to be measuring?
- **Why it matters:** A broken scale can be highly reliable (always says you weigh 150 lbs) but completely invalid (you actually weigh 170 lbs). Scientific research requires both.

## Verified Technical Facts

*(Conceptual lesson - Academic publishing facts)*
- The gold standard for quantitative causal claims is the Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT).
- Qualitative sample sizes are driven by "data saturation" (when new interviews yield no new information), not statistical power calculations.
- Triangulation (using multiple methods or data sources) is a key strategy in mixed methods to increase validity.

## Suggested Hooks & Motivation

- **Pain Point:** "Have you ever designed a survey, collected 200 responses, and then realized your data doesn't actually answer your core research question?"
- **Wow Moment:** "Today, we're going to learn how to reverse-engineer your research design. I'll show you how starting with the correct *question* automatically selects the correct *methodology*."
- **Real-World Scenario:** "Imagine you are researching a new educational app. A quantitative approach will tell you *that* test scores went up by 15%. But only a qualitative approach will tell you *why* the students actually enjoyed using it. To build a great product, you need both."
