## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

1. **Apply** a structured extraction template to pull comparable data points across multiple papers.
2. **Construct** a synthesis matrix (concept matrix) that organizes findings by theme rather than by paper.
3. **Distinguish** between a summary-based literature review ("Paper A said X, Paper B said Y") and a synthesis-based review ("Three studies converge on X, while two challenge it with Y").
4. **Identify** patterns of agreement, disagreement, and gaps across a body of literature.

---

## Key Concepts

### Concept 1: The Cardinal Sin of Literature Reviews
- **What it is:** Most first-draft literature reviews read like book reports — a sequential list of summaries. "Smith (2020) found X. Jones (2021) found Y. Lee (2022) found Z." This is a *summary*, not a *synthesis*.
- **Why it fails:** Your committee and reviewers don't care what each paper says individually. They care about what the body of literature says *collectively*. What are the patterns? Where do experts agree? Where do they disagree? What hasn't been studied at all?

### Concept 2: The Extraction Template
- **What it is:** A standardized table you fill in for every paper during your reading phase. Columns typically include: Author/Year, Research Question, Methodology, Sample Size, Key Findings, Limitations, and Relevance to My Work.
- **Why it matters:** Without a consistent template, you end up with 50 literature notes that all have different structures, making comparison impossible. The template enforces comparability.

### Concept 3: The Synthesis Matrix (Concept Matrix)
- **What it is:** A table where the ROWS are themes/concepts and the COLUMNS are papers. Each cell contains a brief note about what that paper says about that theme. This forces you to think thematically rather than paper-by-paper.
- **How to build it:**
  1. Read 10–15 papers using your extraction template.
  2. Identify 4–6 recurring themes across your extractions.
  3. Create the matrix. Place themes on the left, papers across the top.
  4. Fill in each cell. Some cells will be empty — that's data too (it means the paper didn't address that theme).

### Concept 4: The Four Synthesis Moves
When writing your literature review from the matrix, use these four intellectual moves:
1. **Convergence:** "Three studies (Smith 2020; Jones 2021; Lee 2022) independently found that..."
2. **Divergence:** "However, Patel (2023) challenges this finding, arguing that..."
3. **Gap:** "Notably, none of the reviewed studies addressed the impact of X on Y."
4. **Evolution:** "Early work focused on X (2015–2018), but the field has shifted toward Y (2020–present)."

## Suggested Hooks & Motivation

- **Pain Point:** "Your advisor returns your first literature review draft with one devastating comment in the margin: 'This reads like a list of summaries, not a synthesis. Rewrite it.' You have no idea what the difference is."
- **Wow Moment:** "What if I showed you a single table — the Synthesis Matrix — that transforms a chaotic pile of 50 papers into a structured argument you can write from in an afternoon?"
- **Real-World Scenario:** "The difference between a literature review that gets your thesis proposal approved on the first try and one that gets sent back for major revisions is almost always this: synthesis vs. summary."
